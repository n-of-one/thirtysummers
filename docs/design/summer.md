# Summer

This document describes one summer: how long it lasts, the player's status,
the barriers on the map, what is gathered and how it gets home. The numbers
and the barriers for the first five summers are in
[../current/five-summers.md](../current/five-summers.md).

## Summer duration

A summer is a clock. It starts at camp with a clean slate and a full
hydration bar, and it stops hard wherever the character is standing. How
long a summer lasts depends on the character's age: about five minutes at
15, fifteen minutes at the physical peak from summer 10 to 20, and back down
toward five by the end of a life.

When the clock stops, whatever the character is carrying is stored as if
they had brought it home. So the last minute is never wasted, and a final
trip out is fine. Ending away from camp does cost extra gold in winter, to
pay for fetching the character. The last minute is drawn as dusk, and away
from camp a notice says so and points the way home.

The player can end a summer early from camp, with a button or a key, once
there is nothing left worth doing. A summer can be paused, and it pauses by
itself when the window loses focus.

**Time is what a summer spends.** Rough ground is slow, and its two kinds
are slow in different ways: the character pushes through underbrush at half
speed and wades through mud at under a third. They are separate barriers, so
they have to feel different to walk through. Underbrush comes in degrees:
thin where a clearing gives way to it, full further in, and dense near the
trees. Thin underbrush is faster to walk, and walking wears every kind except
dense underbrush even thinner (see *A trail is worn, not built*, below).

Every improvement is an action whose duration is its price. A cut costs time
once and saves time on every crossing after it. All speed improvements come
from changing the map: a cut path, a bridge, a trail worn by walking the
same way again.

Nothing else runs down. There is no stamina. The clock already does that
job: it never recovers, it is always on screen, and it ends the summer. A
second budget next to it was one the player could not read. The character's
age changes only the length of the summer.

## Player status

**Hydration** decides how far from water the character dares to work. It
drains at a steady rate whatever the character does. It is refilled only at
springs: drinking spots on the stream bank, marked by reeds. The character
drinks standing beside one, with a short action, and every spring refills at
the same speed. Springs sit along the streams, many of them on both banks,
and round the edge of ponds, and never in mud or where mud is the only way
to them. The river has none: it runs in a ravine, and the cliff keeps
everyone off it.
At a spring the action key drinks while the character is thirsty, and lays a
bridge tile when they are not. Water is never carried. When the character is
dehydrated, the view narrows into fog.

Near camp, on camp's side of the first stream, hydration is no concern.
Further out, distance from water is a barrier of its own. So a map has few
streams and ponds further out, and one in a far part of the map is a
relief.

**A tired summer** follows a winter whose upkeep was not paid. Every action
that takes time takes one and a half times as long, and rough ground is
even slower.
Easy ground is walked as fast as ever, so the tiredness is felt where the
work is, never on the way there. Camp restores nothing, because there is
nothing to restore.

## Barriers and keys

A barrier is anything the character cannot afford yet. The character can be
short of four things:

- **time**, for a barrier that takes long to get through;
- **a tool**, such as the axe, which is bought;
- **materials**: a bridge needs sticks and vines from behind another
  barrier;
- **hydration**: a place is too far from water until a well is dug there.

Thickness is the main way to tune a barrier. A thicket 3 tiles deep is a
first-summer job. The same thicket 12 tiles deep, at a few seconds a tile
plus the walk to it, is a two-summer project, because those minutes compete
with everything else the summer could gather. Cuts last, so the second
summer starts where the first one stopped.

The kinds of barrier so far:

- **Underbrush** only slows you down, and less the thinner it is. Walking
  over it wears it into a trail, so the barrier gives way along the route the
  player takes.
- **Dense underbrush** is as slow as full underbrush, and walking never
  wears it. It is not a wall, so there is nothing for the knife to cut. It is
  the floor of a wood and grows near the trees, so a way through a wood stays
  slow however often it is walked.
- **Mud** only slows you down. It does not block; it annoys and it costs
  time, and both count. It does stop the cart, until planks cover it. Mud
  lies in damp hollows and gathers along stretches of the stream, in the
  open and in underbrush, but never on the dense floor of a wood.
- **Thicket** is a wall of brambles, cut with the knife one tile at a time.
- **Stream** is bridged one tile at a time, with sticks and vines.
- **Saplings** are a copse of young trees, felled with the axe for logs.
  Full-grown **trees** are a wall that no knife or axe can fell.
- **Distance from water** is crossed by digging a well.

Rock is the border of the map, and nothing crosses it. Later barriers will
need tools that the structures make: metal from the mine is for tools that
break what no knife or axe can.

## What is gathered

Gold is currency. It is never found in the ground.

**Slot value rises the further out you go.** When the player decides to turn
for home, they are weighing a full pack. So the thing that has to get better
further out is how much one slot brings back. A slot of feathers from the
near ring is worth 5 gold: five feathers to a slot, at 1 gold each. A slot of
shells, behind more barriers than anything the first summers reach, is worth
6. The price of a kind is fixed: a shell is worth the same wherever it was
picked up, and nothing is priced by how far out it was found.

**Depletion pushes the family outward, not price.** A field that has been
worked out is gone, the ordinary kinds come back by half, and the near ring
pays the upkeep and little more. So every gold beyond what the family needs
to get through the winter is further out, and the prices do not have to say
so as well. The earlier answer was prices that doubled with each barrier,
from before a field could be worked out. Why it was dropped is in
[../archive/decided-against.md](../archive/decided-against.md).

**Density makes a far field worth the walk.** A place is worth the trip when
a lot of it stands in one spot, so a field is gathered rather than swept. A
far field with its kinds spread thin is a long walk with more walking inside
it.

The things that only sell are ordinary ones: feathers and shells. Kinds that
have a use do not sell at all. Sticks, vines and logs pay for building, and
winter pays no gold for them. So the way to earn is always a field, never
the material lying at camp. Bulky kinds take two backpack slots. There is no
rarity for now.

**Some kinds grow in one place instead of scattered.** Fruit grows under a
tree, several to a tree, and each one is gathered like anything else. A tree
can be seen from across the ring, so going for food means going somewhere,
and the ring's food is a handful of stops rather than a sweep of the whole
ring. A tree in the near ring that was picked bare has fruit again next
summer. A fruit tree stands where trees grow: in dense underbrush if there
is any, with its fruit on whatever ground is around it. It needs room, with
no other tree within two tiles, so every fruit can be walked to and none is
hidden under a neighbour's canopy.

**Some kinds lie on the ground instead of growing there.** A feather is one.
It is drawn like a dropped item, small and on a shadow, and a press picks it
up as soon as it is in reach, with nothing to wait for. Gathering a vine
means prying it out of the mud, which takes time; a feather is only bent
down for. The game shows this with the same art and the same key it uses for
anything else lying on the grass. Keeping the action key pressed picks up
every feather that comes in reach, so a feather field is walked through with
the key down, the way a row of fruit is worked through. Items the player
dropped are the exception: each one needs its own press, because it was
dropped on purpose to make room.

## Tools and gear

The knife is owned from the start. Every other tool is bought from the town
in winter. Some can only be made once the family brings particular things
back, so gold is not the whole answer. There is one tier of tools, and they
do not wear out. Gear changes costs instead of adding new tools: boots that
make mud faster, a bigger backpack with more slots that slows the walk.

## Improvements

Improvements are made in place, during the summer, with an action on the
tile in front of the character.

- **Cut** a thicket tile with the knife. It leaves underbrush, which walking
  wears into a path like any other.
- **Fell** a sapling with the axe, for a log.
- **Bridge** a stream tile, with a stick and a vine.
- **Well**, dug where there is no water, from logs and sticks. It works like
  a spring, so the family can work further from any stream.
- **Planks**, laid on mud one to a tile, make ground the cart can cross. The
  town saws them from logs.

**A trail is worn, not built.** Walking over underbrush treads it down one
stage at a time, on exactly the tiles the feet crossed. It is the one
improvement with no action and no cost: the route the player already takes
becomes the fast route, so the trip that felt like repetition is the trip
that paid for the road.

- A tile counts as walked over once the feet have left it, so fresh
  underbrush is always crossed at its own speed.
- The first walk already treads it down: it looks trodden and is a little
  easier to push through. The player sees their own line behind them on the
  first trip and can choose to walk it again.
- Each later walk wears it flatter and faster, until it is flat and walked
  at full speed. Flat is still underbrush, pressed down, so the player's
  paths still read as paths, and a tired summer does not slow them.
- One more walk over a flat tile turns it into grass. It is no faster, but
  the path is finished.
- The map starts with some underbrush already partly worn, where it thins
  into a clearing, and walking carries on from there.
- Only underbrush wears. Dense underbrush never does. Mud does not wear into
  anything, because mud is what planks are for. A thicket is a wall, so
  cutting it stays the knife's job.
- Nothing grows back over a trail yet. Thicket creep does take back a cut
  tile next to thicket, walked or not, and a trail through it goes with it.

A trail is also what [winter.md](winter.md) means by a trail forming. What a
cart does to a trail is open while carts are being rethought.

The cache, a box built in the field, is parked in [ideas.md](ideas.md),
because the cart does the same job.

Cutting and felling use tools, and act on whatever is in front of the
character. Everything else is **built, and the player chooses what to build
instead of the game guessing**. A build menu lists what the character knows
how to build and what each one costs. Once the player has chosen, the tile
in front does that one thing until the choice is changed or cleared. Builds
that cannot be paid for yet are still listed, because the menu is where the
player reads the recipes.

The game used to guess what to build from what was in the pack, and that
did not work. When three sticks meant a cache and nothing else, a player
saving for a well was offered a cache on every patch of grass they passed.
They had no way to say which build they meant, and no way to get rid of one
stick. Choosing also keeps the screen quiet: with nothing chosen, open
ground shows no prompt at all. The only building prompt the game shows
unasked is at the water's edge, where the stream is the barrier. It names
the price of a bridge tile and the key that starts one.

A choice ends with the summer, and the clock keeps running while the menu is
open: choosing what to build is part of what a summer is spent on.

What wears, and how fast, is in [winter.md](winter.md).

## The map you have walked

The valley is too big for a player to keep in their head, and a summer that
starts on a map that looks just like last summer's cannot feel like
uncovering anything. So the character keeps a map. It shows only ground that
has been seen at some point in some summer: a circle round the character, a
little wider than the fog lets them see clearly. Everything else is blank.
The map fills in over a life, and it is the one record of where the family
has been.

It shows the terrain and what stands on it: ground and water, walls, camp,
bridges, every place to drink (springs and wells alike) and the landmarks. A
fruit tree counts as a landmark. It is marked in the colour of its fruit
whether or not the fruit has been picked, because a tree worth coming back
to is a place. Trails are not drawn: worn underbrush is still underbrush
until it has been walked into grass. What there is to pick is not shown.
Remembering that the far bank had feathers is up to the player; a map that
marked every feather and shell would answer the question that exploring is
about.

The map sits in a corner as a circle round the character, and looking at it
costs no time. A key swaps the view for the whole valley as the family has
seen it, with the list and the pack still over it. The summer does not stop
for this, so looking at the whole map is still paid for in time.

**The way back to water is always on the map.** When the nearest place to
drink the family has seen is outside the corner circle, a mark on the
circle's edge points straight at it: where the water is remembered to be,
not the way there. Once it is inside the circle, it is drawn there itself.

**Dehydration shrinks the map as well as the view.** As the fog closes in,
the corner map shrinks to what the character can see, though places to
drink nearby stay on it. The whole-valley map fades until only its landmarks
are left: the river, the thickets, the places to drink, the fruit trees and
camp. A dehydrated player can still find water and the way round, but not
the ground in between.

**Landmarks are how the map is read.** A valley of the same grass everywhere
cannot be remembered, so each part of it has one thing that is only itself:
a dead tree, a pair of boulders, a pond, a waterfall in a bend. There is
nothing to pick there and nothing to build. Landmarks are on the drawn map,
and they are how a player says "past the dead tree" to themselves.

## Getting it home

The backpack is the limit: ten slots, with bulky things taking two. It keeps
its contents through the winter, so the player must always be able to empty
it, whatever is in it.

Feathers are the exception to one item per slot: five fit in one slot. They
are light, and carrying the first summer's feathers home one pack at a time
was a slog, not a choice. The stack has a limit, so a feather field still
takes several trips, just fewer. Shells are not light and take a slot each.

**The pack is drawn as a strip** across the bottom of the screen, one cell
per slot, in the order things were picked up. What went in first is on the
left and stays there, and a kind that runs out and is found again goes back
in at the end. Each item shows as its own icon, the same size as on the map,
and a log fills two cells. The empty cells show how full the pack is, with
no number. A stack's cell shows its count, so feathers are the only kind
with a number on them.

**Camp takes everything and sells nothing.** Feathers, shells, fruit and
building material are all stored at camp. Everything is sold in winter,
where the haul is shown with what it earns. A summer has no gold in it.
Fruit stored at camp is winter food, and the player can take it back out at
any time; every fruit taken out is one fewer for winter. Whatever is still
carried when the clock stops is stored too.

**A press stores the whole pack; a long press opens the transfer panel.** At
camp, and at the cart once there is one, a press of the action key stores
everything that place will take, so a normal arrival is one press. A long
press opens the transfer panel: the pack on one side and what that place
keeps on the other, moved one kind at a time. The key that opened the panel
closes it.

**What is kept where over a winter.**

- **The ground** keeps nothing. Anything dropped is gone by spring.
- **The cart** keeps everything except fruit, which rots wherever it is
  left.
- **Camp** keeps everything, and it is the only place where fruit becomes
  winter food.

A heap in a field is scattered by a year of rain and animals, a cart is a
box, and fruit rots unless it is at camp. So these rules read as ordinary
physics rather than game rules.

**Dropping** is for when the pack is full in a field and there is nothing to
spend on. It is one key, not a screen. The pack always has one kind
selected; when that kind runs out, the selection moves to whatever takes the
most slots. One key drops all of the selected kind, and another moves the
selection along the strip. Dropped items land one to a tile, spreading
outward onto open ground, so the scatter shows honestly how much was
carried. A drop is refused only when there is no open ground nearby at all.
When the pack is full, the prompt names the kind and the count. That is the
only safeguard against throwing winter food on the grass, and it is enough,
because dropped items can be picked back up.

Picking up a dropped item is a press, not an action that takes time:
gathering is prying a vine out of the mud, picking up is bending down. A
dropped item is drawn as a smaller version of itself on a shadow, so it is
never mistaken for one still growing. Ending a summer away from camp stores
what is carried, never what was dropped.

**The cart is storage on wheels.** It has room for any amount, and it takes
the same press and long press as camp. Parked at the edge of a field, it is
where the pack is emptied; pushed home, it brings the haul with it. The
character pushes it by walking into it. It moves at walking speed on grass,
bridges and planks, crawls through underbrush, and will not go into mud or
thicket. Pushing it makes the character sweat, so hydration drains faster
while pushing. A route the cart is pushed along often wears into a trail
over the years, which is how its road gets made without a tool; mud needs
planks.
